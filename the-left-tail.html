Tail 00 · Orientation

One framework, five lectures

This course does something unusual: it measures the risk of every instrument — stocks, bonds, options, forwards, futures — with the same three objects. Learn those three and the rest is bookkeeping.

The whole course in three symbols

Every instrument gets decomposed into a weight vector w over a set of risk factors. The factors have a mean vector μ and a variance-covariance matrix Ω. Then:

μP = wTμ   σP2 = wTΩw   VaRα = μP + zα σP The only thing that changes between lectures is how you build w. Everything after that is identical.
Excel=MMULT(TRANSPOSE(w),mu) + NORMSINV(alpha)*SQRT(MMULT(MMULT(TRANSPOSE(w),Omega),w))

There is only one formula. The exam questions are almost entirely about building w correctly.

Two conventions this course uses — and they are not the textbook's

VaR is NEGATIVE here
This course writes VaRα = μ + zασ with z negative, so VaR comes out as a negative return or a negative amount — "VaR(1%, 1 day) = −1.4521%". Hull and most textbooks quote VaR as a positive loss. Follow the lecturer's sign and label the number, and never mix the two on one page. One class workbook (the equity portfolio sheet) does flip it with a leading minus, so check which sheet you are in.
The mean μ stays in
Most VaR courses set μ = 0. This one does not — μ is estimated from the data and carried through. That is why the slides show a hypothesis test for whether μ is significantly different from zero, and why one class sheet reports both "VaR" and "VaR with Zero Mean".
Legacy Excel names
Every class workbook uses NORMSINV, NORMSDIST, STDEV and COVAR, not the modern dotted names. They still work. The XL lab lists the modern equivalents and the one place where the old and new versions genuinely differ.

Tail 01 · Financial Institutions

Where the risk comes from

Every activity a financial institution does generates its own kind of risk. Market risk is one of nine — and the reason it gets measured so precisely is that it is the one that can bankrupt you in a week.

The sixty-second version
  1. Pick a business line and the risk follows from it. Security trading → market risk; lending → credit; process → operational.
  2. Bankruptcy is a statement about the left tail: the firm fails when the loss exceeds equity, rA < −E₀/A₀.
  3. Read that forwards for a default probability, backwards for the capital a target rating needs. The price of safety is wildly non-linear in the rating.
  4. Regulation writes the rules; supervision checks that a particular firm follows them. BIS for banks, IOSCO for securities, IAIS for insurance — and Basel wants at least 250 business days of data, which is why every class workbook has 250 rows.
PD = NORMSDIST((−E₀/A₀ − μ_A) / σ_A)

Bankruptcy is a statement about the left tail

This is the bridge from lecture 1 to everything that follows. The balance sheet gives A0 = D0 + E0. One period later the institution is bankrupt if assets fall below debt — equivalently, if the loss exceeds equity.

bankrupt ⟺ Ã1 < D0 ⟺ r̃A < − E0A0 So the probability of default is the left-tail probability of the asset return, cut at minus the equity ratio. That is a VaR statement read backwards.
ExcelPD = NORMSDIST((-E0/A0 - mu_A) / sigma_A)  ·  required equity ratio = -(mu_A + NORMSINV(PD)*sigma_A)

Run it the other way and you get required capital: pick the default probability you are willing to live with, and the equity ratio follows. The deck gives the target probabilities as one-year default rates by rating.

    The five checks that catch almost every lost mark

    Is VaR negative?
    This course writes VaR = μ + z σ with z negative. A positive answer means you dropped the sign or used the wrong z. Label it: "VaR(1%, 1 day) = −1.4521%".
    Do σ, y and T share one time unit?
    Daily σ with an annual y is the single most expensive slip on the option questions. The deck's own example uses 5% per month with T = 12 months.
    Is w in the same order as Ω?
    The option workbook stores dy first, rS second. Nothing will error — you will just get a plausible wrong number.
    wrS − wdy/T = 1
    Free and exact for any call, put or forward. With T = 1 — every class example — the two weights simply differ by 1.
    Σ component VaR = portfolio VaR
    Euler's theorem. If the components do not add up to the total, the Ωw column is wrong. And a futures price must sit between the PV of its cash flows and their undiscounted sum.

    Tail 02 · Market Risk

    VaR and expected shortfall

    Standard deviation tells you how much things move. It does not tell you how much you can lose. VaR answers the second question, badly but usefully — and expected shortfall fixes the one thing about it that is actually broken.

    The sixty-second version
    1. Everything downstream is a return. Price is linear in the return, so if y = a + b·r then μy = a + bμ and σy² = b²σ² — that one rule carries the whole course.
    2. VaRα = μ + zασ: the worst return you keep with confidence 1 − α. Negative here, and the mean stays in.
    3. Estimate μ and σ from at least 250 days, then test whether μ is worth keeping: t = μ̂√T/σ̂.
    4. VaR can fail subadditivity — two books can be riskier apart than together. Expected shortfall never does: ES = μ − σ·φ(zα)/α.
    5. Changing horizon: the mean scales with T, the volatility with √T. Never scale the VaR itself.
    VaR = μ + z·σ  ·  ES = μ − σ·φ(z)/α  ·  z₁% = −2.326348

    Returns first, because everything downstream is a return

    discrete:  r̃ = P̃1 + D − P0P0   continuous:  r̃ = ln P̃1 + DP0 =(P1+D-P0)/P0 versus =LN((P1+D)/P0). Over one day these agree to about four decimal places; over a year they do not.

    The deck makes a point that looks trivial and is not: price and return are linearly related. Rearranged, r̃ = −1 + D/P0 + (1/P0)P̃1, which is of the form a + b·P̃1. That matters because of the linear-transformation rule for normals:

    if ỹ = a + b r̃ and r̃ ~ N(μ, σ) then μy = a + bμ  and  σy2 = b2σ2 This single rule is what licenses every weight vector in the course. Note σy2 = b²σ² — the constant a drops out of the variance.
    Why this mattersEvery later lecture builds a return as a linear function of risk factors and then applies this rule. Bond return = −Dmod·dy. Option return = Δ(S/Op)rS + ρ(1/Op)dy. Both are just ỹ = a + b r̃ in disguise. If you can see the linearity you can write the VaR.
    =LN(C4/C3)

    =(D29-D28)/D28
    But the additional questions workbook uses simple returns for stocks. Both appear in the course. State which you used — and never mix them in one covariance matrix.
    =(B4-B3)/100

    What VaR actually claims

    Value at Risk is the possible maximum loss over a given horizon at a given confidence level. The deck's own reading is the one to memorise, because it names the horizon and the frequency in the same breath:

    "At 99% confidence, the loss is 8% for a one-year investment" means that for a one-year horizon, the chance of losing more than 8% is one time in a hundred such investments.

    z̃ = x − μσ ~ N(0,1)   rα = μ + zασ   VaR = (μ + zασ) · W0 zα = NORMSINV(α). W₀ is the money invested. The output is negative — that is this course's sign convention.

    Fine printThe law of large numbers says μ̂ → μ and σ̂ → σ as T grows. The deck immediately warns why that is not the end of the story: return behaviour may differ from expected behaviour — the process itself changes.     Is μ worth keeping? The t-test

    Because the mean is so small relative to the volatility over one day, it is worth asking whether it belongs in the VaR at all. The deck tests H0: μ = 0.

    t = μ̂ √Tσ̂ Note σ̂ here is the standard deviation, not the variance — the deck's own example makes that unambiguous.
    t = √10 × 0.0289√0.002106 = 0.0913830.045891 = 1.9914 10 observations, average return 0.0289, variance 0.002106. The deck's figure reproduces exactly.
    Slide checkTwo problems on this slide. The degrees of freedom should be T − 1 = 9, not 8. The deck compares against 2.31, which is the two-tailed 5% value for 8 df; for 9 df it is 2.2622 (=T.INV.2T(0.05,9)). The conclusion is unchanged, because 1.9914 is below both. And the deck's concluding sentence is garbled — it reads "we cannot reject the null hypothesis that the expected return is significantly different from zero". The null is that the mean is zero, so the correct statement is: we cannot reject the null that μ = 0, so the mean is not significantly different from zero.
    Concept checkSo can you set the one-day mean to zero? Statistically, usually yes. But this course keeps μ anyway, and you should follow it. Watch what μ does to the sign: in the coupon-bond example μ̂B comes out positive, so including it makes VaR less negative — the zero-mean version is the more conservative one there. In the option example μ̂ is negative and including it makes VaR worse. The mean does not reliably cut one way.

    Expected shortfall, and why VaR is technically broken

    Expected shortfall is the expected loss given that the loss exceeds VaR. Also called conditional VaR, C-VaR, or tail loss. The deck's key claim: two portfolios with the same VaR can have very different expected shortfalls, because VaR looks at one point on the tail and ignores its shape.

    VaR = μ + σ zα   ES = μ + σ e−zα2/2√(2π) α =B5*EXP(-1*(NORMSINV(1-alpha))^2/2)/(SQRT(2*PI())*alpha) — the class workbook's own form. Use SQRT(2*PI()), not 22/7.
    Fine print from the deckThe multiplier term is always positive, so with μ = 0 the ES formula returns a positive number even though it represents a loss. The deck's instruction is explicit: when μ < 0, write ES = μ − σ·e−z²/2/(√(2π)α) so that the sign matches the VaR convention. Mind the sign on this one every single time — it is the most commonly botched formula in the deck.

    A coherent risk measure is the amount of cash you must add to a portfolio to make its risk acceptable. Four properties:

    PropertyMeaningVaRES
    Monotonicitya always-worse portfolio must measure riskier✓✓
    Translation invarianceadding cash K cuts the measure by K✓✓
    Homogeneityscaling the book by λ scales the measure by λ✓✓
    Subadditivitymerging two books cannot raise total risk✗✓
    Why this mattersVaR satisfies three of the four. Failing subadditivity means a bank could cut its measured risk by splitting one desk into two — which is exactly backwards, and is why regulators have moved toward ES for market risk capital.

    Changing the horizon

    VaRT-day = VaR1-day √T   EST-day = ES1-day √T The deck's condition, stated exactly: losses in successive days are independent, normally distributed, and have a mean of zero.
    Common slipRead that third condition again — mean of zero. This course does not set μ = 0, so the whole VaR cannot simply be multiplied by √T. The class workbooks get this right, and the formula they use is the one to copy: =mu*T + NORMSINV(alpha)*sigma*SQRT(T). The mean scales with T and the volatility with √T.     How you find out the model was wrong

    Count the days on which the actual loss exceeded the VaR. At 99 % confidence you expect an exception one day in a hundred — about 2.5 days a year. Far more than that and the model is understating risk; far fewer and it is wasting capital. Regulators formalise this into zones that scale a bank’s capital multiplier, which is the practical reason the number has to be right rather than merely conservative.

    Two subtleties worth a sentence in an answer. First, exceptions should be independent — four in one week is a worse sign than four spread over a year, because it means the model missed a regime, not a bad day. Second, backtesting a one-day VaR is honest; backtesting a ten-day VaR against overlapping ten-day windows is not, because the windows share data.

    Bonds, duration and the curve

    A bond's price cannot be used directly, because its maturity shrinks every day. So the risk factor is the yield, duration is the translator, and a coupon bond becomes a portfolio of zero-coupon bonds — one per cash flow.

    The sixty-second version
    1. You cannot estimate a bond’s return series, because yesterday’s price belongs to a bond with one more day to run. Model the yield instead.
    2. One Taylor term turns a yield change into a return: rB = −Dmod·dy.
    3. A coupon bond is a portfolio of zero-coupon bonds, one per cash flow, each discounted at its own spot rate. Its weights are wt = (PVt/B) × (−t).
    4. After that it is the same three lines as every other instrument: μP = wTμ, σP² = wTΩw, VaR = μP + zσP.
    5. Marginal and component VaR then say which position carries the risk, and the components add up to the total exactly.
    w_t = (PV_t / B) × (−t)  ·  D_mod = D_mac/(1+y)

    Why you cannot just take the return on a bond

    The deck opens with two bonds: Bond X, five years when issued with five years left, and Bond Y, also five years when issued but with two years left. How do you estimate their expected returns and volatilities?

    You cannot use a price history, because time to maturity reduces as the bond approaches maturity. Yesterday's price and today's price belong to different instruments. A five-year bond's return series is contaminated by the fact that it was a 5.004-year bond last week.

    Deeper: what the linear approximation leaves out

    Hull RMFIConvexity

    The term duration throws away

    rB = −Dmod·dy is the first Taylor term. The second is convexity, C = (1/B)·d²B/dy², which under continuous discounting is just Σt²PVt/B. The fuller statement is ΔB/B ≈ −DmodΔy + ½C(Δy)².

    Put the deck’s own bond in and you can see why it is ignored. The five-year 3% bond has C = 23.04. A two basis-point yield change — a typical day in the class data — moves the price 0.0923% through duration and 0.000046% through convexity: three orders of magnitude apart. Push the move to 200 basis points and the same two terms are 9.23% and 0.46%, so convexity is now worth 5% of the answer. Convexity is a stress-test correction, not a one-day-VaR correction — and that is exactly the argument that lets an option be reduced to Delta and Rho.

    The sign is worth knowing on its own: convexity is positive for an ordinary bond, so duration alone always overstates the loss from a rise in yields and understates the gain from a fall.

    Hull RMFICash-flow mapping

    Why real desks do not use one factor per cash flow

    This course gives every cash-flow date its own risk factor, which is exact and perfectly manageable for a five-year bond. A trading book with thousands of bonds cannot do that — the covariance matrix would be enormous and most of it would be estimated from almost no data.

    The standard fix is to choose a fixed set of standard vertices (1m, 3m, 6m, 1y, 2y, 5y, 10y…) and split each actual cash flow between the two neighbouring vertices, keeping its present value and its variance unchanged. Every bond in the book then loads onto the same short list of factors, and Ω stays small.

    It matters for an exam only as a sentence: the method here is the exact case of a technique that is normally applied approximately.

    Options as portfolios

    An option's price depends on the underlying and on the interest rate, so it has two risk factors and a two-element weight vector. Build it two ways — from the Greeks, or from an equivalent stock-and-bond portfolio — and they agree.

    The sixty-second version
    1. Gearing is the whole problem. A call costs a fraction of the stock, so the same move in the stock is a far bigger move in the option — the option’s VaR is a multiple of the stock’s.
    2. Price it with Black-Scholes, then take two Greeks only: Delta for the stock, Rho for the rate.
    3. That is the weight vector: w = [Δ·S/Op ; ρ/Op] over the factors [rS ; dy].
    4. Or build the equivalent asset: an option is ΔS of stock plus B of bond, so w = [ΔS/Op ; −(B/Op)·T/(1+y)]. Both routes agree to four decimals.
    5. Free check: wrS − wdy/T = 1, always.
    w = [Δ·S/Op ; ρ/Op]  ·  Δ_C = N(d₁), ρ_C = TKe^(−yT)N(d₂)

    Deeper: the two Greeks the course leaves out

    Hull OFODGamma

    What two Greeks cannot see

    Reducing an option to Delta and Rho is a first-order statement: it assumes the option’s value moves in a straight line with the stock. It does not. The curvature is Gamma = φ(d₁)/(Sσ√T), and the fuller expansion is ΔOp ≈ Δ·ΔS + ½Γ(ΔS)².

    The consequence is not that the VaR is slightly off — it is that the shape of the P&L distribution changes. Because (ΔS)² is always positive, a long option position has a right-skewed P&L: the delta-normal number overstates its risk. A short option position is skewed the other way and delta-normal understates it, which is the dangerous direction and the reason Hull spends a chapter on the delta-gamma correction.

    For the one-day horizons in this course the correction is tiny, exactly as convexity is for bonds. Say that sentence rather than ignoring the issue.

    Hull OFODVega

    The risk factor this course does not model

    σ enters every formula on this page as a constant estimated from history. It is not constant. Vega = S√T·φ(d₁) measures what an option is worth per unit of volatility, and for the class example it is 542.62 — a one percentage-point rise in σ is worth about 5.4 on an option priced at 83.41.

    Compare that with the two factors the course does model and the omission is stark: volatility risk is the same order of magnitude as rate risk for an at-the-money option. A complete measurement would add σ as a third risk factor with its own row in Ω. The decks leave it out, and the only defensible reason is that the VaR horizon is one day.

    The same logic explains implied volatility: quote an option price and invert Black-Scholes for the σ that reproduces it. Plot implied σ against strike and it is not flat — the smile is the market saying the normal assumption behind this whole course is wrong in the tails.

    Contracts worth nothing

    A futures contract has no value when you enter it, so it has no percentage return. That breaks every formula so far — and the fix is to work the whole thing in money instead.

    The sixty-second version
    1. A forward is long one call + short one put struck at the delivery price, so VF = C − P and the option machinery carries straight over.
    2. A futures contract is worth nothing at inception. There is no percentage return to model, so the weights are in money and the VaR comes out in money.
    3. Second decomposition: futures ≈ long the asset, borrow its spot price. w = [S₀ ; T·S₀].
    4. On a zero-coupon bond, w = [−(T+N)B₀ ; +T·B₀]. On a coupon bond, one row per post-delivery cash flow plus a single financing row — and that row carries a factor of T.
    5. The deck’s two largest errors are on this lecture. Both are flagged where they appear.
    w = [S₀ ; T·S₀] — in money  ·  ₀F_T = S₀·e^(y_T·T)

    Cost of carry, and the weight vector in money

    Borrow S0 at y for T periods, buy the asset, hold it. Net cash flow today is zero; at T you owe S0eyTT and hold the asset. No arbitrage forces:

    0FT = S0 eyTT   example: 100 × e(3%/12)×6 = 100 × e0.015 = 101.5113 =100*EXP(0.03/12*6). The deck's 101.51.

    Now decompose. Fu = +1 S − 1 B, where BT = S0eyTT and B0 = S0. Differentiating the bond leg:

    dB = −T B0 dyT = −T S0 dyT  ⟹  dFu = dS − dB = dS + T S0 dyT Two minus signs make a plus. A long futures position GAINS when rates rise, because it is short a bond.
    dFu = dSSS + T S0 dyT = S0 rS + T S0 dyT  ⟹  w = S0T S0 = 100(6/12) × 100 = 10050 Factors are r_S and dy_6M. The weights are in MONEY, so VaR comes out in money, not percent.
    The ruleThe deck asks, and answers: "Can we find % return for a futures contract?" Everything about futures VaR is done in money units: w in money, μ and Ω on the raw factors, and VaR in Baht. This is the one lecture where VaR is not a percentage.

    The sixty-second version
    1. All nine workbooks share one eight-step skeleton. Steps 1–4 and 7–8 never change; only the instrument and its weights do.
    2. The class files use the legacy function names — NORMSINV, NORMSDIST, STDEV, COVAR. They still work, but COVAR is the population version while STDEV is the sample one, which is why the two disagree in the fifth decimal.

    The custom function the workbooks depend on

    Not standard Excel{=varcovar(F5:H255)} → builds the whole variance-covariance matrix from a block of factor columns

    This is a VBA user-defined function supplied with the course files, which is why they are saved as .xlsm and why macros must be enabled. It is not available in a blank workbook. Three ways to reproduce it:

    Data → Data Analysis → Covariance
    The Analysis ToolPak. Fast, but it writes a lower-triangular block of static values using the population divisor, and it does not update when the data change.

    Array formulas: the keystrokes that decide whether you get a number or a matrix

    Ctrl+Shift+Enter
    The deck says this explicitly for MMULT. In Excel 2019 and earlier, an array formula entered with plain Enter returns only the top-left element. No error, just a wrong number.
    Select the output range first
    To see a 3×3 MMULT result you must select 9 cells, type the formula, then press Ctrl+Shift+Enter. Selecting one cell gives you one number.
    {braces} in the formula bar
    Excel adds them itself to show a formula is an array. Typing braces yourself does not work — they become text.
    Microsoft 365 dynamic arrays
    Results spill automatically and CSE is unnecessary. A spill blocked by existing content gives #SPILL!. This is why the class files, written in an older Excel, still carry CSE instructions that you may not need.
    σP far too small?
    Check CSE first. A missing Ctrl+Shift+Enter on the nested MMULT silently returns w₁²Ω₁₁ instead of wᵀΩw, which for a diversified book is a small fraction of the truth.

    Every function used in the course, by job

    NORMSINV · NORMSDIST
    The quantile and the CDF. zα for VaR, N(d₁) and N(d₂) for Black-Scholes, and the probability of a loss.
    AVERAGE · STDEV · COVAR · CORREL
    μ, σ, and Ω. CORREL does not appear in the class sheets but is how you sanity-check a covariance: ρ = Cov/(σ₁σ₂) must sit in [−1, 1].
    LN · EXP
    Log returns, continuous discounting, and d₁. Continuous compounding is used throughout the option and futures lectures.
    SQRT
    σ from a variance, √T scaling, and σ√T inside d₁.
    MMULT · TRANSPOSE
    μP = wᵀμ and σP² = wᵀΩw. Nest two MMULTs for the quadratic form.
    SUMPRODUCT
    Macaulay duration from periods and PV weights, in one cell. Also a TRANSPOSE-free alternative: =SUMPRODUCT(w,MMULT(Om,w)).

    Goal Seek · Solver

    The trap list

    −1.96 for a 95% VaR
    −1.96 belongs to α = 2.5%. A 95% VaR wants −1.644854. Using −1.96 overstates it by 19.2%. The lecture-3 portfolio slide prints this error.
    Forgetting the /100 on dy
    Spot rates are quoted in percent. =(B5-B4)/100. Omit it and every bond VaR is 100× too large.
    σ where the label says σ²
    The lecture-3 T-bill slide labels two standard deviations as variances. If a "variance" is around 0.0009 on daily yield changes, it is a standard deviation.
    w order ≠ Ω order
    The option workbook puts dy first, rS second. MMULT will not complain if you reverse w — it will pair the stock weight with the yield variance and return a plausible number.
    Missing Ctrl+Shift+Enter
    Returns the top-left element of the matrix product. The classic symptom is a portfolio σ that is far too small.
    Mixing continuous and discrete
    Continuous PV goes with duration −t; discrete PV goes with −t/(1+yt). The coupon-bond sheet mixes them and overstates VaR by 2.3%.
    22/7 for π
    Makes the density, and therefore Vega and Gamma, 0.02012% too small. =SQRT(2*PI()).
    Scaling the whole VaR by √T
    The mean scales with T, the volatility with √T. =mu*T+NORMSINV(a)*sd*SQRT(T).
    Percentage return on a futures contract
    Its initial value is zero. Work in money: w = [S₀ ; T·S₀].
    STDEV^2 beside COVAR
    n−1 on the diagonal, n off it. Pair VAR.S with COVARIANCE.S.
    Annual σ with T in months
    σ, y and T must share one period. The deck's own example uses 5% per month with T = 12 months.
    LOG instead of LN
    LOG is base 10 in Excel and scales d₁ by 2.3026.
    Coupons before delivery in a futures price
    Only post-delivery cash flows belong in 0FT. And the price must sit between their PV and their undiscounted sum.
    Forgetting T in the financing row
    A coupon-bond-futures weight vector's last element is +T×Σ(X·B), not Σ(X·B). The lecture-5 slide drops the T.
