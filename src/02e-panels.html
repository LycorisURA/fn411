Tail 05 · VaR for forwards and futures

Contracts worth nothing

A futures contract has no value when you enter it, so it has no percentage return. That breaks every formula so far — and the fix is to work the whole thing in money instead.

Two decompositions, and why each is used

Deck 05Forward

Long one call + short one put

Both struck at the forward price 0FT. The payoffs match in every state, so the risks match. Useful because you already know how to risk-decompose an option.

Deck 05Futures

Long the asset + borrow its spot price

The cost-of-carry portfolio. Useful because it turns a zero-value contract into two instruments that each have a value and a known exposure.

The ruleIdentical payoffs in every state means identical price and identical risk. So VF = C − P at t = 0, and the forward's weight vector is the call's minus the put's, scaled by each leg's share of VF. This is the deck's definition of equivalent assets; the related idea of a dominant asset — higher payoff in one state, no worse in the others — is what makes an arbitrage argument work.

Cost of carry, and the weight vector in money

Borrow S0 at y for T periods, buy the asset, hold it. Net cash flow today is zero; at T you owe S0eyTT and hold the asset. No arbitrage forces:

0FT = S0 eyTT   example: 100 × e(3%/12)×6 = 100 × e0.015 = 101.5113 =100*EXP(0.03/12*6). The deck's 101.51.

Now decompose. Fu = +1 S − 1 B, where BT = S0eyTT and B0 = S0. Differentiating the bond leg:

dB = −T B0 dyT = −T S0 dyT  ⟹  dFu = dS − dB = dS + T S0 dyT Two minus signs make a plus. A long futures position GAINS when rates rise, because it is short a bond.
dFu = dSSS + T S0 dyT = S0 rS + T S0 dyT  ⟹  w = S0T S0 = 100(6/12) × 100 = 10050 Factors are r_S and dy_6M. The weights are in MONEY, so VaR comes out in money, not percent.
The ruleThe deck asks, and answers: "Can we find % return for a futures contract?" No. The contract's value at inception is zero, so there is nothing to divide by — a percentage return would be division by zero. Everything about futures VaR is done in money units: w in money, μ and Ω on the raw factors, and VaR in Baht. This is the one lecture where VaR is not a percentage.

Zero-coupon bond futures

Now the underlying is itself rate-sensitive, so there are two yields: the one that discounts the underlying bond (maturity T+N) and the one that finances the carry (maturity T).

0FT = e−yT+N(T+N) × eyTT = e−yT+N(T+N) + yTT Buy the long bond, borrow for T. =EXP(-y_TN*(T+N)+y_T*T)
dFu = −(T+N) B0 dyT+N + T B0 dyT  ⟹  w = −(T+N)+T × B0 B₀ is the underlying ZCB's price today, e^(−y_(T+N)(T+N)). Opposite signs: the long bond loses when its own yield rises, the short financing leg gains when the funding yield rises.

Concept checkThe two weights do not cancel: −1.3874 + 0.9250 = −0.4624. A long bond-futures position is net long duration, because the underlying bond is longer-dated than the financing. The gap is exactly N × B₀ = 0.5 × 0.924963 — the extra six months of exposure you are buying. N is the source of the residual risk, which is why a futures hedge can never be perfect unless the contract's underlying matures exactly when the contract delivers.

Coupon bond futures, and the second big error

A coupon bond is a portfolio of zeros, so a coupon-bond future is a portfolio of ZCB futures — one per post-delivery cash flow. Only cash flows after delivery belong in the futures price.

0FT = { CT+1e−yT+1(T+1) + … + (CT+N + 100)e−yT+N(T+N) } × eyTT Equivalently, discount at the FORWARD rates: 0F_T = ΣCF·e^(−(T)f(k)·k), where (T)f(K) = [y_(T+K)(T+K) − y_T·T] / K.

The deck's example: futures on a coupon bond, T = 1 year, FV = $100, C = 4% p.a. paid semiannually, and the underlying has 2 years left at delivery. Periods are half-years, so delivery is period 2 and the four remaining coupons fall at periods 3, 4, 5 and 6.

Slide checkThe deck reports the bracket as 119.61 and the futures price as 121.42. Both are impossible: the undiscounted post-delivery cash flows total only 108 (2 + 2 + 2 + 102), so no positive discount rate can produce 119.61, and carrying forward for one year at 1.5% cannot get 108 to 121.42. Recomputing term by term gives 97.531938 and a futures price of 99.0059. The deck's multiplier e0.0075×2 = 1.0151 is correct, and 119.61 × 1.0151 = 121.42, so the two printed figures are internally consistent — the error is upstream, in the bracket.
Fine printThe sanity check to use on any futures-price question: the futures price must lie between the PV of the post-delivery flows and their undiscounted sum, here between 97.53 and 108. A figure above the undiscounted total is arithmetically impossible whenever rates are positive. This one check would have caught the slide.

Why this mattersNote the units: this VaR is −3.9136 Baht, not a percentage, because the futures weights are in money. Against the bond's −3.8389 Baht the two are strikingly close, which is what makes the hedge work. But the answer to the deck's question — can the remaining net risk be completely eliminated? — is no. The bond loads on dy₁…dy₅ while the futures book loads on dy₂…dy₆ and dy₁ through the financing leg. Six factors, five contracts, and a different loading pattern: you can minimise the residual but not zero it. That residual is basis risk.
