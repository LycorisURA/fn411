Tail 04 · VaR for options

Options as portfolios

An option's price depends on the underlying and on the interest rate, so it has two risk factors and a two-element weight vector. Build it two ways — from the Greeks, or from an equivalent stock-and-bond portfolio — and they agree.

Why this mattersThe deck's framing of option risk is gearing. A call on one share costs far less than the share, so a small percentage move in the asset produces a much larger percentage move in the option. Gearing amplifies returns on the upside and applies equally to the downside — an option's value can reach zero very fast. That is why the weight vectors on this panel come out as numbers like 9.47 and −10.45 rather than fractions: an option is a hugely levered position on its factors.

The deck's route to VaR: an option's two major determinants are the underlying price and the interest rate, so its market risk is the price risk of the underlying plus interest rate risk. Treat the option as a portfolio of stock and bond.

Black-Scholes-Merton, in the course's notation

The stock price is lognormal: dS/S = μdt + σdz̃, so rS ~ Normal(μ − σ²/2, σ²).

Workbook checkThree of the four class option calculators compute the normal density as =EXP(-(d^2)/2)/SQRT(2*22/7). 22/7 = 3.142857 is not π = 3.141593. Because it sits in the denominator, the density comes out 0.02012% too small, and so do Vega and Gamma, which are the only outputs that use it. On the 1382.41 example, Gamma 0.00213928 should be 0.00213971 and Vega 542.51655 should be 542.62572. The digital option sheet already uses SQRT(2*PI()) correctly, so the fix is a one-character edit. Delta, Rho, and both option prices are unaffected — they use NORMSDIST, not the density — so every VaR on this panel is fine.

Two Greeks, not five

This course needs exactly two: the sensitivity to the underlying and the sensitivity to the rate. Gamma, Vega and Theta appear in the calculator but not in the VaR.

dOp(S,y) = ∂Op∂SdS + ∂Op∂ydy = Delta × dS + Rho × dy A first-order Taylor expansion in two variables. The second-order terms — gamma — are dropped, which is the approximation this whole method rests on.

Concept checkThe class workbook orders the vector dy first, then rS, because the Ω matrix is built from columns laid out as dY1 then rS. The order of w must match the order of Ω, or MMULT will silently pair the stock weight with the yield variance. It will not error. Check the labels every time.

The building block route: option = stock + bond

The deck's alternative, which it calls the LEGO approach. Two blocks: the underlying asset, and a zero-coupon bond. In a one-period binomial world you can solve for the exact holdings that replicate the option, because two states give two equations in two unknowns.

C1u = ΔS1u + B(1+y)   C1d = ΔS1d + B(1+y) Two equations, two unknowns Δ and B. Subtract to eliminate B.
Δ = C1u − C1dS(u − d)   B = C1d(1+u) − C1u(1+d)(1+y)(u − d)   u = eσ − 1, d = e−σ − 1 =(J25-J26)/(H23*(H21-H22)) and =(J26*(1+H21)-J25*(1+H22))/((1+H19)*(H21-H22)) in the class sheet.
rC = ΔSC rS + BC rB = ΔSC rS + BC(−T1+y) dy  ⟹  wCT = [ ΔSC , −BCT1+y ] If the bond is priced continuously as e^(-y_T T), the duration is T rather than T/(1+y).

Fine printA one-step binomial is a crude pricer. On the additional-questions put (strike 1,050, T = 1) the binomial gives 0.7864 while Black-Scholes gives 3.5367 — a factor of four and a half. Yet the two VaRs come out at −10.7423% and −10.7474%, within 0.005 percentage points. The weight vector is far more robust than the price, because the gearing and the price errors largely cancel in the ratio.

Greeks by numerical differentiation

Some options have no closed-form Greeks. The deck's answer is a central difference:

dydx ≈ y(x + Δx) − y(x − Δx)2 × Δx  as Δx → 0 =(E4-E5)/(2*$C$2) in the class sheet. Two revaluations per Greek, one up and one down.

Why this mattersThe deck's sanity check is worth repeating because it makes the method obvious: for y = 2x + 1 with x = 10 and Δx = 0.1, the central difference gives (21.2 − 20.8)/0.2 = exactly 2, which is the true derivative. For a linear function the central difference is exact at any step size; for a curved one, a step that is too large picks up curvature and a step that is too small loses precision to rounding. ΔS = 1 on a spot of 1,382 is about 0.07% — a good default.
Fine printThe deck also gives cash-or-nothing digital options, which pay $1 in the money and have closed forms: C = e−yTN(d₂) and P = e−yTN(−d₂). Their deltas and rhos come from the same machinery, and the class digital sheet confirms the numerical and analytical rho agree to seven digits — 0.6739535 for the call.

The ruleCash has zero risk and zero return, so its row in Ω is all zeros. σPort = √(0.5²×0.3² + 0 + 0) = 15%, exactly half. Halving a risky position halves both μ and σ, so VaR halves too — a linear lever, unlike diversification.
delta hedge:  dPortfolio = dOp − Delta × dS = Delta × dS − Delta × dS = 0 Hold −Delta shares against a long option. VaR_α = z_α |Delta × S/Op| σ_rS is the unhedged figure this removes.
rho hedge ratio = − Rho−T e−yTT = RhoT e−yTT Held in a zero-coupon bond with FV = 1 and maturity T, because dB = −T e^(−yT) dy is the bond's own rate exposure.

Concept checkThe deck's four notes are the summary of the whole course. Portfolio return is the weighted average of asset returns. Cash has zero risk. Assets with the same source of risk — a stock and an option on it — can be offset exactly, which is delta hedging. Assets with non-zero correlation can only be partly offset, which is diversification. Hedging removes risk; diversification dilutes it.
