Tail 03 · VaR for fixed income

Bonds, duration and the curve

A bond's price cannot be used directly, because its maturity shrinks every day. So the risk factor is the yield, duration is the translator, and a coupon bond becomes a portfolio of zero-coupon bonds — one per cash flow.

The sixty-second version
  1. You cannot estimate a bond’s return series, because yesterday’s price belongs to a bond with one more day to run. Model the yield instead.
  2. One Taylor term turns a yield change into a return: rB = −Dmod·dy.
  3. A coupon bond is a portfolio of zero-coupon bonds, one per cash flow, each discounted at its own spot rate. Its weights are wt = (PVt/B) × (−t).
  4. After that it is the same three lines as every other instrument: μP = wTμ, σP² = wTΩw, VaR = μP + zσP.
  5. Marginal and component VaR then say which position carries the risk, and the components add up to the total exactly.
w_t = (PV_t / B) × (−t)  ·  D_mod = D_mac/(1+y)

Why you cannot just take the return on a bond

The deck opens with two bonds: Bond X, five years when issued with five years left, and Bond Y, also five years when issued but with two years left. How do you estimate their expected returns and volatilities?

You cannot use a price history, because time to maturity reduces as the bond approaches maturity. Yesterday's price and today's price belong to different instruments. A five-year bond's return series is contaminated by the fact that it was a 5.004-year bond last week.

The ruleModel the yield, not the price. Yields for a fixed maturity are comparable across days — the one-year spot rate today and the one-year spot rate yesterday describe the same point on the curve. The deck's alternative for a direct return series is the Zero Rate Return (ZRR) index, which behaves like a price index of a constant-maturity zero-coupon bond.
B0 = FT(1 + yT)T ≈ exp(−yTT) × FT   coupon: B0 = C1(1+y1)1 + … + CT + FT(1+yT)T Each cash flow is discounted at its OWN spot rate, not at a single yield to maturity. That is what makes it a portfolio of zeros.
Fine printThe spot rate is the current interest rate on a zero-coupon bond maturing at a specific time. The deck distinguishes the yield curve (par/coupon-bond yields) from the zero yield curve (spot rates), both published by ThaiBMA, and the bond pricing above needs the zero curve.

From Taylor to duration in four lines

The deck derives the price-yield link from a first-order Taylor expansion. Take continuous compounding and a $1 face value, so B(yT) = exp(−yTT):

dB = d exp(−yTT)dyT dyT = −T exp(−yTT) dyT = −T B0 dyT Differentiate the exponential, and the whole bond price reappears as a factor.
rB = dBB0 = −T B0 dyTB0 = −T dyT  ⟹  VaRα = zα σ(rB) = zα T σ(dyT) The bond price cancels completely. For a continuously-compounded zero, duration IS maturity.

Under discrete compounding the same derivation gives a factor of 1/(1+y), and the two duration measures separate:

Dmac = Σ t wt   Dmod = 11 + y Dmac   dBB = −Dmod dy w_t is the PV weight of cash flow t. =SUMPRODUCT(periods, PV_weights) gives Macaulay in one cell.
μB = −Dmod μy   σB2 = Dmod2 σy2   VaRα = −Dmod μdy + zα Dmod σdy The linear-transformation rule from lecture 2, with b = −D_mod. Note the mean flips sign and the variance does not.
=SUMPRODUCT(A10:A12,D10:D12)
Macaulay duration in periods, from a column of period numbers and a column of PV weights. Divide by the frequency for years.
=A10/(1+H3/2)
The class sheets' per-cash-flow modified duration: period ÷ (1 + semiannual rate). One per row, then weighted.
Continuous or discrete? Pick one.
With continuous discounting the weight multiplier is −t. With discrete discounting it is −t/(1+yt). The class workbooks are not consistent about this — see the slide check below.

A coupon bond is a portfolio of zeros

This is the central idea of the lecture. Each cash flow is its own zero-coupon bond, exposed to its own point on the curve. The bond's return is the PV-weighted average of those returns.

rB = w1rB1 + w2rB2 + … + wTrBT   wt = 1B0 CFt(1 + yt)t   rBt = −Dtmod dyt So the ADJUSTED weight that multiplies dy_t is w_t × (−duration_t). That single product is what the exam is testing.

Because every rBt is a multiple of a dy, the covariances collapse neatly:

Var(rBi) = Var(−i·dyi) = i2 Var(dyi)   Cov(rBi, rBj) = (−i)(−j) Cov(dyi, dyj) The durations come out of the covariance as plain multipliers — which is exactly why you can fold them into w instead and keep Ω as the raw yield-change matrix.
μP = wTμ   σP2 = wTΩw   VaRα = wTμ + zα√(wTΩw) The deck's own Excel instructions: 1. TRANSPOSE(Array) 2. MMULT(Array1, Array2) then press Ctrl+Shift+Enter.
=MMULT(TRANSPOSE(w),mu)

A portfolio of basic assets: bond plus stock

The deck now mixes asset classes. Portfolio P = B + S, with the bond priced continuously. Differentiating and dividing through gives a return that is linear in three factors — two yield changes and one stock return.

rP = wB F1e−y1B(−1) dy1 + wB F2e−2y2B(−2) dy2 + wS rS So w = [w_B × (PV₁/B) × (−1) ; w_B × (PV₂/B) × (−2) ; w_S]. Three numbers, one per risk factor.

Marginal and component VaR

With an amount xi in component i, the deck defines two derivatives of the portfolio VaR:

marginal VaRi = ∂VaR∂xi   component VaRi = xi ∂VaR∂xi In Excel: marginal = z/sigma_P * MMULT(Omega, w) ; component = marginal × weight (or × position value).
Class sheetmarginal: =B14/B22*MMULT(F9:G10,C9:C10)  ·  component: =B30*C9  ·  check: =SUM(components) must equal portfolio VaR

Three properties the deck states:

≈ incremental VaR
Component VaR is approximately the change in portfolio VaR from removing that position entirely. Approximately, not exactly — the derivative is local.
Σ components = total VaR
Euler's theorem. Because VaR is homogeneous of degree one in the position sizes, the components add up exactly. This is the property that makes allocation possible, and it is a free arithmetic check on your sheet.
A sensible allocation
So component VaR is how you charge risk to desks. Standalone VaRs do not add up and would over-charge everyone.
Fine printIncremental VaR is not the same as component VaR. The class equity sheet computes incremental VaR separately as =VaR_P − VaR_without_i — for the MS/ATT book that is 1,620,113.82 − 367,850 = 1,252,263.82 for MS, against a component VaR of 1,436,389.57. Both are correct answers to different questions. The assignments panel works this example in full.

Deeper: what the linear approximation leaves out

Hull RMFIConvexity

The term duration throws away

rB = −Dmod·dy is the first Taylor term. The second is convexity, C = (1/B)·d²B/dy², which under continuous discounting is just Σt²PVt/B. The fuller statement is ΔB/B ≈ −DmodΔy + ½C(Δy)².

Put the deck’s own bond in and you can see why it is ignored. The five-year 3% bond has C = 23.04. A two basis-point yield change — a typical day in the class data — moves the price 0.0923% through duration and 0.000046% through convexity: three orders of magnitude apart. Push the move to 200 basis points and the same two terms are 9.23% and 0.46%, so convexity is now worth 5% of the answer. Convexity is a stress-test correction, not a one-day-VaR correction — and that is exactly the argument that lets an option be reduced to Delta and Rho.

The sign is worth knowing on its own: convexity is positive for an ordinary bond, so duration alone always overstates the loss from a rise in yields and understates the gain from a fall.

Hull RMFICash-flow mapping

Why real desks do not use one factor per cash flow

This course gives every cash-flow date its own risk factor, which is exact and perfectly manageable for a five-year bond. A trading book with thousands of bonds cannot do that — the covariance matrix would be enormous and most of it would be estimated from almost no data.

The standard fix is to choose a fixed set of standard vertices (1m, 3m, 6m, 1y, 2y, 5y, 10y…) and split each actual cash flow between the two neighbouring vertices, keeping its present value and its variance unchanged. Every bond in the book then loads onto the same short list of factors, and Ω stays small.

It matters for an exam only as a sentence: the method here is the exact case of a technique that is normally applied approximately.
